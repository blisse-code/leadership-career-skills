## 1. Level calibration

Read the target level first, because the same achievement is framed differently per level. The "proof" column is what the resume must make obvious on page 1.

| Level | Core question the reader asks | Proof that answers it | Typical scope line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lead / Senior-plus | Can they own a product area and lift others? | Owned a full product or vertical, led a workstream, mentored, set a local standard | "Design lead for checkout and payments, 3 designers, 2 squads" |
| Principal / Staff (IC) | Does their judgment shape decisions beyond their team? | Cross-team frameworks, design system or platform direction, decisions they changed, orgs that adopted their patterns | "Principal across 4 product lines, 30+ designers and engineers consuming the system" |
| Manager | Did they build a team that ships consistently high-quality work? | Hiring, coaching, rituals, quality bar, delivery reliability, retention | "Managed 6 designers and 1 researcher" |
| Director | Did they scale design and align cross-functionally? | Multiple teams, managers of managers or multi-discipline, operating model, roadmap influence, cross-functional alignment, budget | "Led 3 teams (18 people) across product, research, content" |
| Head of Design / Experience | Did the whole practice improve, and did the business notice? | Built or rebuilt the function, org design, maturity shift, design-business link, exec partnership | "Head of a 25-person experience practice, reporting to CPO" |
| VP / SVP | Did they influence executive decisions and tie design to outcomes? | Revenue, retention, cost, market position; board or C-suite decisions shaped; multi-org budgets | "VP Design, 60 people, USD 8M budget, member of product leadership team" |
| Chief Design / Experience Officer | Did they turn design into competitive advantage? | Company strategy, brand and product coherence, measurable competitive shift | "CDO, design + brand + research, 120 people" |

Company context shifts the level: a "Head of Design" at a 40-person startup is a player-coach who still ships; at a bank it is an org builder with governance duties. Read the JD for tells: "hands-on", "IC", "player-coach" vs "build the function", "operating model", "budget".

For VP-and-above readers (CEO, CFO, CPO, board), frame each role as **mandate** (what you walked into: cost pressure, stalled growth, transformation), **business impact** (revenue, retention, efficiency, position), and **leadership leverage** (who you hired, how you structured the team, how you raised the bar and earned exec trust). They evaluate risk, leverage, and ROI, not craft.

## 2. The eight leadership dimensions

Use these to tag evidence and to check that each recent role shows at least three.

| # | Dimension | What it proves | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business outcome | Design moved a number the business cares about | Conversion, revenue, retention, NPS/CSAT, cost-to-serve, support tickets, time to market |
| 2 | Team and org building | Built the capability, not only the output | Hired N, grew team from X to Y, promotions, retention, career ladder, org restructure |
| 3 | Operating model | Changed how work gets done | Rituals, critique, design review gates, DesignOps, research ops, design system governance, cycle time |
| 4 | Cross-functional influence | Managed up and across | Roadmap changes won, exec sponsorship, shared metrics with product/eng, conflict resolved |
| 5 | Strategy and vision | Set direction others followed | Experience strategy, north-star, multi-year roadmap, maturity shift (NN/g levels) |
| 6 | Quality bar and craft | Kept standards high at scale; player-coach | Design system adoption, accessibility compliance, heuristic scores, personally led a critical flow |
| 7 | Innovation and AI | Brought new capability safely | AI-assisted workflows, AI features with guardrails, new service lines, prototyping speed |
| 8 | Talent growth and culture | People got better because of the leader | Mentees promoted, workshops run, internal academy, psychological safety measures, engagement scores |

Julie Zhuo's lens is a fast sanity check: a manager improves outcomes through **purpose** (why), **people** (who), **process** (how). A leadership resume with only process bullets reads like a project manager; with only purpose bullets it reads like a visionary who never shipped.

IxDF's direction lens: managing **up** (resources, exec buy-in), **across** (product, engineering, business), **down** (hiring, coaching, performance). Strong leadership resumes show all three.

## 3. Evidence-mining questions

People under-report leadership because it doesn't feel like "work". Ask these, grouped so the user can answer quickly. Offer example answers.

**Mandate**
- What was broken or at stake when you arrived? (e.g. "no design system, 4 inconsistent apps, NPS falling")
- Who hired you and what did they expect in year one?

**Scale**
- Largest team you led directly? Indirectly (including vendors, agency partners)?
- Budget, number of products, markets, users, revenue under your area?
- Most senior stakeholder you worked with regularly?

**Moves**
- What decision did you push that others resisted? How did it end?
- What process or ritual did you create that still runs?
- Who did you hire or grow that is now senior? Any promotions?
- What did you stop the team from doing?
- Where did you personally do the craft work because it was critical?

**Outcomes**
- What number moved? From what to what, over what time? If no number: what observable change (fewer escalations, faster sign-off, adopted by N teams)?
- Any awards, client renewals, contract expansions, pitches won?
- What did executives or clients say (quotes from reviews, emails)?

**Proof sources**
- Performance reviews, promotion packets, award submissions, QBR decks, case studies, LinkedIn recommendations. These are gold for evidence and usually contain numbers the user forgot.

## 4. Leadership signature

The pattern others rely on the user for, stated as something a stranger could verify. It powers the headline and summary.

Find it with three questions: What do you already lead without the title? What do people come to you for? What value do you bring beyond pixels?

| Weak (claim) | Strong (signature) |
|---|---|
| "Visionary design leader" | "Turns fragmented multi-market products into one system teams can ship on" |
| "Passionate about people" | "Builds design teams that clients renew for: 3 accounts extended after team rebuilds" |
| "AI enthusiast" | "Brings AI into design workflows with guardrails: internal academy, 2 production AI features" |

## 5. Principal / Staff IC leadership

Leadership without direct reports is still leadership. Adapt Will Larson's staff archetypes:

- **Tech lead analog (design lead)**: guides a group's execution and quality.
- **Architect**: owns the direction of a critical domain (design system, IA, platform patterns).
- **Solver**: parachutes into the hardest problems across the org.
- **Right hand**: extends an executive's reach, runs org-wide initiatives.

Scope line = what the user's judgment shaped: "Influenced 4 product lines and 30+ designers; owned design system direction." Bullets emphasise decisions changed, standards adopted, people mentored, and craft on the most critical surfaces.

## 6. IC-to-leader transition

For a first leadership role, the resume must show "already doing the job":
- Lead with the leadership signature.
- Pull leadership-without-title evidence to the top of each role: led a workstream, ran critiques, mentored N designers, influenced roadmap priorities without authority, onboarded hires, facilitated workshops, created a framework others use.
- Use "led", "set", "aligned", "coached" only where true; otherwise "facilitated", "proposed and drove", "partnered with".
- Micro-problems count when framed with before/after (built the team's intake process; cut rework).
- Consider a "Leadership highlights" block under the summary.

## 7. Red flags hiring managers look for

- No cross-functional evidence (design in a vacuum).
- Solutions without the problem or mandate.
- No business impact anywhere.
- Isolated feature work, no ecosystem or scale.
- Every bullet says "I" for team outcomes, or every bullet says "we" and the user's role vanishes.
- Tool lists and self-rated skill bars at leadership level.
- Titles inflated relative to scope lines (reference checks catch this).
- Adjective-heavy summary ("results-driven, strategic, passionate").
