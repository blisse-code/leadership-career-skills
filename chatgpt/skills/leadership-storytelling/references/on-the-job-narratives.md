# On-the-Job Narratives

## 1. Vision story (team, all-hands, strategy launch)
Frame: Ganz Self > Us > Now, or Duarte what is / could be.
1. A specific moment from customers or the team that shows the gap (not a statistic first).
2. What we share: why this team cares.
3. What could be: a concrete picture of a user's day when we get it right.
4. The path: 3 moves, not 10.
5. The ask: what each person does Monday.
Test: could a team member retell the vision in one sentence at lunch?

## 2. Pitching design investment to executives (CEO, CFO, CPO)
Frame: Answer first + SCQA + options.
1. Recommendation and the ask in one line (money, headcount, time, scope).
2. Situation and complication in business terms (revenue, churn, cost-to-serve, risk).
3. Evidence: 2-3 facts (data, one customer moment).
4. Options: do nothing, minimum, recommended; cost, return, risk each.
5. What success looks like, how it will be measured, when you'll report back.
6. Pre-empt the objection ("I know you're thinking this is a nice-to-have...").
Anchor claims in known research where helpful (e.g. McKinsey 2018: top-quartile design performers grew revenue 32 points faster over five years), but lead with the company's own data.

## 3. Change / transformation narrative
1. Honour the past ("the way we worked got us here").
2. What changed outside (market, customers, tech, AI).
3. Voice the resistance explicitly (Simmons: I know what you're thinking).
4. The new way, with one early win already achieved.
5. What stays the same (reduces fear).
6. What happens next and how people get support.

## 4. Delivering bad news (up or down)
Fact first in one sentence; impact; cause (no blame); what we're doing; what I need; when you'll hear next. Do not bury the fact in context. Own your part.

## 5. Handling resistance in the room
- Name the concern better than the objector can.
- Separate the concern from the proposal ("the risk you're pointing at is real; here's how the pilot contains it").
- Offer a small, reversible test rather than winning the argument.

## 6. Critique and feedback stories (values in action)
Use a real story of a critique that changed the work to set norms: what was said, how it was received, what shipped. Teaches the team what "good critique" looks like better than a rules list.

## 7. Recognition stories
Specific person, specific action, specific effect. "Ana rewrote the error states after sitting with call-centre agents; complaints about failed payments dropped the next month." Public, concrete recognition is one of the cheapest leadership tools.
